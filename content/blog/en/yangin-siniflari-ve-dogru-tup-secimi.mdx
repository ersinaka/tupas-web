Not every extinguisher works on every fire. Using the wrong one can make a fire worse or put the person using it at risk. Choosing correctly starts with understanding **fire classes**.

## Fire classes

The European standard EN 2 classifies fires by what is burning:

| Class | Burning material | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Solids | Paper, wood, fabric, plastics |
| **B** | Flammable liquids | Petrol, diesel, paint, solvents |
| **C** | Flammable gases | Natural gas, LPG, propane |
| **D** | Combustible metals | Magnesium, aluminium swarf, lithium |
| **F** | Cooking oils | Fryer and pan oils |

Fires involving electrical equipment are not a separate class, but they require a non-conductive extinguishing agent.

## Which extinguisher for which fire?

- **ABC dry powder:** Class A, B and C fires. The most common extinguisher because it is multi-purpose. Powder can reduce visibility indoors and needs cleaning up.
- **CO2 (carbon dioxide):** Class B and electrical fires. Leaves no residue, ideal for switchboards and server rooms.
- **Foam:** Class A and B fires. Blankets the liquid surface and helps prevent re-ignition.
- **Water:** Class A fires only. **Never** use on electrical or oil fires.
- **Metal powder:** Special powder for Class D metal fires.
- **Wet chemical:** Class F cooking oil fires. Used alongside hood suppression systems in commercial kitchens.

<Callout>
Never use water on an oil fire. The water flashes to steam inside the hot oil and throws burning oil out, spreading the fire.
</Callout>

## Practical advice by space

- **Office:** ABC powder units, plus CO2 for server and electrical rooms.
- **Restaurant kitchen:** Hood suppression system, a fire blanket and a kitchen-rated portable extinguisher.
- **Warehouse and workshop:** Higher-capacity ABC powder units; foam where flammable liquids are stored.
- **Fuel station and car park:** Foam and wheeled powder units.
- **Metalworking:** Class D metal powder extinguisher.
